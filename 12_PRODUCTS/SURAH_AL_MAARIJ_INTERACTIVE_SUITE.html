Al-Nadr's Cynical Inquest: Sa'ala Sa'ilun bi-'Adhabin Waqi'

In canonical Sunni exegesis recorded by Imam at-Tabari, Al-Wahidi (Asbab an-Nuzul, p. 291), and Ibn Kathir on the authority of Abdullah ibn Abbas, the opening of Surah Al-Ma'arij descended regarding Al-Nadr ibn al-Harith al-'Abdari.

Al-Nadr stood before Quraysh in Mecca and openly challenged divine authority, proclaiming: 'O Allah! If this Qur'an is indeed the ultimate truth from You, then rain down upon us stones from the sky, or bring upon us a painful torment!' (Surah al-Anfal 8:32).

Allah revealed: 'A questioner asked about an inevitable torment [bi-'adhabin waqi'] for the disbelievers, which none can repel.'

This torment struck Al-Nadr in this world when he was captured at the Battle of Badr and executed for his severe war crimes and persecutions, followed by his eternal retribution in the Fire.

Authorities: Tafsir al-Tabari (23/595); Al-Wahidi, Asbab an-Nuzul, p. 291; Sahih al-Bukhari 4649; Tafsir Ibn Kathir (8/218).

The Cosmic Dilation of Time: The 50,000-Year Day & The Lightness for Believers

Ayah 4 states: 'The angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a Day the measure of which is fifty thousand years.' Classical theologians note that this indicates vast cosmic time dilation: what requires 50,000 human years of physical travel is traversed by angelic beings in a single divine Day.

In Sahih Muslim (987) on the authority of Abu Hurairah, the Prophet ﷺ explained that every hoarder of wealth who refuses to pay its due zakah will be tormented by it on a Day whose length is fifty thousand years, until judgment is decreed between all servants.

Yet for the sincere believer, this terrifying duration is compressed into effortless joy. In Musnad Ahmad (3/75) from Abu Sa'id al-Khudri, when the companions asked in terror about a Day lasting 50,000 years, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ smiled and said: 'By Him in Whose Hand is my soul, it will be lightened for the believer until it is easier upon him than a single prescribed prayer he prayed in the world.'

Temporal experience in the Akhirah is entirely subjective: horror elongates seconds into millennia, while divine peace renders fifty thousand years like a fleeting moment of contemplation.

Authorities: Sahih Muslim 987; Musnad Ahmad 11723; Tafsir Ibn Kathir (8/220); Fath al-Bari (11/398).

The Anatomy of Anxiety: Inna al-Insana Khuliqa Halu'a & The Musalloon Exemption

In Ayahs 19–22, Allah provides the supreme diagnostic template of unguided human psychology: 'Indeed, mankind was created anxious and impatient [Halu'a]: when touched by evil, he is panicky [Jazu'a]; and when touched by good, he is stingy and withholding [Manu'a]—except those devoted to prayer [Illa al-musalleen].'

Imam al-Hasan al-Basri explained: 'Halu'a' is the individual whose desires are volatile; if a minor financial calamity or illness touches him, he loses all faith and falls into despair; but if an abundance of wealth or ease touches him, he forgets God, becomes greedy, and refuses to share with the needy.

The Surah establishes that this secular neurosis cannot be permanently cured by material wealth, psychotherapy, or intellectual prestige; its sole ontological remedy is the eightfold spiritual constitution of 'Al-Musalloon' (70:23–35).

By anchoring life in constant prayer (Da'imoon), institutionalized charity (Haqqun Ma'loom), vivid consciousness of Judgment, sexual chastity, honoring covenants, and uncompromising truth in witness, the believer transcends the panic of loss and the arrogance of gain.

Authorities: Tafsir al-Tabari (23/605); Tafsir al-Qurtubi (18/285); Jami' al-Bayan; Ihya' 'Ulum ad-Deen.

Emerging Like Racers to Idols: Ka-annahum ila Nusubin Yoofidoon

Ayah 43 depicts the resurrection: 'The Day they will come out from the graves rapidly as if they were racing to an idol stone [Ka-annahum ila nusubin yoofidoon].'

Imam al-Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir explain that 'Nusub' (plural of Nasb) refers to the smooth, stone pagan altars erected around the Ka'bah and across Mecca, upon which pagan Quraysh slaughtered sacrificial beasts. Whenever a feast day arrived, pagan tribesmen would sprint with fanatical enthusiasm to be the first to reach and touch the stone.

The Qur'an deploys a devastating visual metaphor: the very frantic, competitive haste that the Meccan oligarchs displayed in running toward lifeless stones is recreated on the Day of Judgment—except now they are driven forward in terrifying humiliation toward the true Sovereign of the Universe.

Their eyes are cast downward in terror ('Khashi'atan absaruhum'), blanketed in catastrophic disgrace ('Tarhaquhum dhillah'), realizing that the idols they ran to have evaporated into dust.

Authorities: Tafsir al-Qurtubi (18/295); Tafsir Ibn Kathir (8/228); Tafsir at-Tabari (23/614); Lisan al-'Arab.
