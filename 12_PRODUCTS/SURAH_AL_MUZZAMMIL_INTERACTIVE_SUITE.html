The Nightly Crucible: Qumil-Layla Illa Qaleela & The One-Year Mandate (Muslim 746)

In Sahih Muslim (746), Sa'd ibn Hisham asked 'A'ishah about the night prayer of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. She replied: 'Did you not recite: Ya Ayyuha al-Muzzammil?' He said: 'Yes.' She said: 'Verily, Allah made standing at night obligatory at the beginning of this Surah.'

'So the Prophet ﷺ and his companions stood in night vigil for an entire year, until their feet became swollen with fluid, and Allah withheld the concluding verses of the Surah in heaven for twelve months.'

'Then Allah revealed the mitigation in the final verse: 'Inna Rabbaka ya'lamu annaka taqoomu adna min thuluthayi al-layl...' and thus the night prayer became voluntary (Tatawwu') after having been an absolute obligation.'

This intense one-year spiritual bootcamp forged the companions' interior depth, anchoring their souls in transcendent presence before the severe political, military, and legal trials of Medina commenced.

Authorities: Sahih Muslim 746; Sunan Abi Dawud 1342; Tafsir Ibn Kathir (8/255); Al-Mustadrak 'ala as-Sahihayn.

Tarteel & The Heavy Word: Wa Rattilil-Qur'ana Tarteela

In Ayah 4, Allah commands: 'And recite the Qur'an with measured recitation [Tarteela].' Ali ibn Abi Talib provided the canonical definition: 'Tarteel is the clear articulation of the letters [Tajweed al-Huroof] and the deep awareness of the stopping places [Ma'rifat al-Wuqoof].'

Ibn Abbas stated: 'To recite a single short Surah like Al-Zalzalah or Al-Qari'ah with slow, reflective Tarteel is more beloved to me than to speed-read through the entire Qur'an without contemplation.'

This contemplative discipline prepares the soul for 'Qawlan Thaqeela' (A Heavy Word). The Qur'an is described as heavy because of its legal obligations, its existential truths, and its literal physical impact during descent: in Sahih al-Bukhari, 'A'ishah noted that on freezing winter mornings, sweat dripped from the Prophet's forehead as revelation weighed down upon him.

Fast, unthinking recitation glides over words; Tarteel allows the celestial weight of the message to shatter complacency and sculpt the human consciousness.

Authorities: Sahih al-Bukhari 2; Tafsir at-Tabari (23/678); Tafsir al-Qurtubi (19/37); Al-Burhan fee 'Ulum al-Qur'an.

The Choking Food & Heavy Shackles: Ta'aman Dha Ghussah

In Ayahs 12–13, Allah describes the destiny of the arrogant oppressors: 'Indeed, with Us are shackles [Ankalan] and a burning fire [Jaheema], and food that chokes [Ta'aman dha ghussah], and a painful torment.'

Ibn Abbas explained that 'Ta'am dha ghussah' refers to the thorny plant of Dari' and the foul fruit of Zaqqum: when swallowed, its sharp spikes lodge in the esophagus; it neither passes downward into the stomach nor can it be vomited back up.

When the sinners choke upon this agonizing substance, they scream for water to wash it down—and are provided with boiling liquid like molten brass that scalds their lips and facial skin off before it reaches their throats.

This retributive imagery was revealed in Mecca to confront affluent pagan magnates who mocked the Prophet while indulging in lavish banquets, reminding them that wealth hoarded without charity culminates in eternal, suffocating entrapment.

Authorities: Tafsir at-Tabari (23/685); Tafsir Ibn Kathir (8/259); Al-Jami' li-Ahkam al-Qur'an (19/45).

The Divine Mitigation: Faqra'oo Ma Tayassara minal-Qur'an

Ayah 20 is the longest and most comprehensive verse in the Surah, articulating divine mercy: 'He knows that there will be among you the sick, and others traveling throughout the earth seeking of the bounty of Allah, and others fighting in the cause of Allah. So recite of it what is easy [Faqra'oo ma tayassara minh].'

Classical scholars note that this verse pairs two essential worldly categories alongside the sacred struggle: the sick battling physical weakness, and the commercial traveler seeking lawful livelihood (Yadriboona fil-ard). This proves that engaging in honest commerce is an honorable station recognized alongside jihad.

Allah does not desire that religion become an unbearable burden that breaks human physical vitality. By reducing mandatory nocturnal standing to 'what is easy', Islam consecrated consistency over exhaustion.

The verse concludes by directing believers toward the permanent pillars of communal elevation: establishing prayer, paying zakah, advancing a goodly loan to Allah, and continually seeking divine forgiveness (Wastaghfiroo Allah).

Authorities: Tafsir Ibn Kathir (8/261); Tafsir al-Qurtubi (19/52); Ahkam al-Qur'an lil-Jassas; Sahih Muslim.
